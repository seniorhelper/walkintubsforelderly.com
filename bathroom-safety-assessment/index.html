Bathroom safety assessment for an aging parent

Before your family spends money on a new tub or shower, it helps to know exactly what is going wrong at bath time. A bathroom safety assessment answers that. Here is what a professional one covers, how to get one through your parent's doctor or local agency, and a free walk-through you can do on your next visit.

Direct answer: A bathroom safety assessment is a walk-through of the room, plus watching how your parent gets in, sits, stands and gets out. An occupational therapist does the professional version after a doctor's referral; Medicare can cover it as outpatient or home health therapy. Start with the free walk-through below, then bring the results to the doctor.

What is a professional bathroom safety assessment?

A home safety assessment is a visit from a trained professional who looks at the room and, more importantly, at the person using it. The National Institute on Aging says that after a fall, a doctor might suggest that an occupational therapist, physical therapist or nurse visit the home to assess its safety and advise on changes that lower the risk of falling.

Of those three, the occupational therapist (OT) is the one families most often want for the bathroom, because bathing, toileting and dressing are exactly the daily tasks OTs train in. A good assessment usually includes:

  • Watching the real routine. How your parent walks into the bathroom, gets over the tub wall or shower curb, sits, washes, stands and dries off. A list of hazards on paper misses the moment that actually matters.
  • Measuring. Tub wall height, door width, the space beside the toilet, seat heights and where walls can take a grab bar.
  • Checking the person, not just the room. Strength, balance, reach, vision, skin and memory all change which equipment will work.
  • Recommendations in writing. Usually a mix of habits, equipment (a tub bench, handheld shower, raised toilet seat) and changes that need an installer.
  • Practice. Many therapists teach a safer way to transfer and let your parent try equipment before anyone buys it.

Why it is worth doing before you shop: the OT can tell you whether your parent can safely sit while a walk-in tub fills and drains, whether a step-in threshold is still manageable, or whether a roll-in shower with a seat is the safer long-term choice. That one answer can save the family from buying the wrong bathroom.

What should you say to the doctor?

Doctors respond to specifics. Bring the printed walk-through results and describe what you have seen, not what you fear. Something like this works well:

"Mom has started sitting on the edge of the tub to swing her legs in, and she's using the towel bar to stand up. She had a near-fall in the bathroom last month. Could you refer her for an occupational therapy evaluation that includes bathing and a home safety check?"

A few points make the appointment more useful:

  • Mention any fall or near-fall. CDC guidance for family caregivers is to tell a health care provider right away if a loved one has fallen, is worried about falling, or seems unsteady.
  • Bring a list of every medicine, including over-the-counter drugs and supplements, and ask whether any could cause dizziness.
  • Ask directly about home health if your parent rarely leaves the house without help. That is the route where Medicare pays in full for covered services.
  • Ask for the recommendations in writing. They help with insurance, grant applications and conversations with siblings, and a doctor's letter is useful documentation if the family later claims a medical expense deduction. Our family paying guide covers that.

How can you prepare for the visit?

  • Be there, in person or on a video call. Parents tend to say they are fine; the therapist needs the family's observations too.
  • Ask your parent to wear what they normally wear and use the bathroom as they normally would. Remove nothing beforehand; the therapist needs to see the real room.
  • Have the walk-through results, the medicine list and any recent hospital or ER paperwork ready.
  • Write down your questions, including "Is a walk-in tub safe for Dad, given that he would sit while it fills and drains?" and "Would a roll-in shower be safer in five years?"
  • If you live far away, read our long-distance caregiving guide for measuring by video call.

Why does the bathroom get its own assessment?

Falls are common and costly in later life. The CDC reports about 4.5 million emergency department visits for falls among older people each year, and nearly 319,000 hospital stays for hip fractures. The bathroom packs many of the classic risks into one small room: water on hard surfaces, a high tub wall, low seats, and hurried trips at night. For figures specific to bathrooms, see Aging Safely Baths' bathroom fall statistics.

The encouraging part is that the bathroom is also one of the easiest rooms to change, and most of the first fixes cost little. An assessment helps the family spend in the right order.

Questions families ask

Who does a bathroom safety assessment for an elderly parent?

Usually an occupational therapist, after a referral from your parent's doctor. The National Institute on Aging notes that a doctor might also send a physical therapist or nurse to assess home safety. Some Area Agencies on Aging and local home modification programs offer assessments too.

Does Medicare pay for an occupational therapy home safety evaluation?

It can. Medicare Part B helps pay for medically necessary outpatient occupational therapy when a provider certifies it is needed; your parent pays 20% after the deductible. Under the home health benefit, covered services cost nothing, but your parent must be homebound and occupational therapy must first be ordered along with nursing, physical therapy or speech therapy.

Will Medicare pay for the grab bars or a new shower the assessment recommends?

Original Medicare generally does not pay for home modifications. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer bathroom safety items as supplemental benefits, and local programs may help low-income homeowners. Check the plan's Evidence of Coverage and call the Eldercare Locator at 800-677-1116.

Can a bathroom remodeling company do the assessment instead?

An installer can measure the room and price changes, often for free, but is not a clinician. Use the installer for measurements and a written quote, and the occupational therapist for what your parent can safely do.

How long does a home safety assessment take?

It varies by therapist and by how many rooms are included. Ask when you book, and ask that bathing and toileting be watched, not just discussed.

What can we do while we wait for the appointment?

Put nonslip strips in the tub, remove loose rugs, clear the tub edge, add night lights, set the water heater to 120 degrees Fahrenheit, and add a tub bench and handheld shower so your parent can wash sitting down.
