Bathroom changes when a parent moves into your home

When a parent moves in, the bathroom is the room most likely to decide whether the arrangement works. It is where they need privacy most and where a fall is most likely. The good news is that the decisions are concrete: which bathroom, how wide the door, how high the toilet, shower or tub, and who pays.

Direct answer: Give your parent a bathroom on the same floor as their bedroom with a door that opens to at least 32 inches clear (the ADA Standards minimum), a toilet seat 17 to 19 inches high, grab bars, and either a roll-in shower ($13,500 to $17,000 installed) or a walk-in tub (about $15,500 installed after the current promotion). If you provide over half their support, the IRS may let you count the medical modifications.

Published November 8, 2026 · Updated November 8, 2026 · By the Aging Safely Baths family guide team

How many families are doing this?

More than most people realize. Pew Research Center's analysis of Census Bureau data found 59.7 million U.S. residents, 18% of the population, living in multigenerational households in March 2021, four times the 1971 count, when the share was 7%. Among people 65 and older, 20% of women and 15% of men lived in such a household. The Census Bureau's narrower definition, three or more generations under one roof, counted 6.0 million households in 2020, 7.2% of family households, up from 5.1 million in 2010.

Most of those homes were built for one generation. Before the truck arrives, walk the bathroom your parent will use with the questions below.

Which bathroom should your parent use?

The one on the same level as their bedroom, with the shortest, clearest path and no stairs at night. Kaiser Permanente Washington's occupational therapy checklist, written for people coming home after joint surgery, asks exactly that question and suggests a bedside commode if the only bathroom is on another floor. Beyond location, prefer the bathroom that:

  • Has a door that can be widened or re-hung if a walker or wheelchair arrives later.
  • Leaves room for a second person beside the toilet and the shower.
  • Is not the one your teenagers share; a parent who feels rushed will skip baths.
  • Can take a night light on the route from the bed (a CDC Check for Safety item).

Will the doorway fit a walker or a wheelchair?

Measure before you promise anything. The ADA Standards, as explained by the U.S. Access Board, require a doorway to provide at least 32 inches of clear width, measured from the door stop to the face of the door when it stands open at 90 degrees. Doorways deeper than 24 inches need 36 inches, and nothing may project into the opening below 34 inches above the floor. Private homes are not covered by the ADA, but 32 inches clear is the figure installers and occupational therapists work from, because it is what a standard wheelchair needs.

Many bathroom doors in older homes fall short once you subtract the door and the stop. Options, from least to most work: offset (swing-clear) hinges, re-hanging the door to swing out, a pocket door, or widening the opening. IRS Publication 502 lists widening doorways among medical modifications, which matters below.

How high should the toilet be?

The ADA Standards put the top of the seat at 17 to 19 inches above the floor, and allow 15 to 19 inches in dwelling units. Higher is easier to stand up from. Quick fix: a raised toilet seat with arms, or grab bars beside the toilet, both on the NIA's home safety checklist. Permanent fix: a comfort-height toilet. The ADA guidance puts grab bars 33 to 36 inches to the top of the gripping surface, with a side bar at least 42 inches long. In a private home an occupational therapist will place them where your parent's hand actually goes; our bathroom safety assessment guide explains how to arrange that visit.

Who pays, and does it change your taxes?

Usually a mix of the parent's savings, siblings splitting the cost, and sometimes a program. One angle is specific to a parent who moves in: IRS Publication 502 (2025 returns) lets you include medical expenses you paid for a dependent, and a parent can be a qualifying relative if, among other tests, you provided over half their support for the year. Grab bars, support bars and widened doorways made for medical reasons do not usually increase a home's value, so their cost can be included in full, but only medical expenses above 7.5% of adjusted gross income count, and only if you itemize. This is not tax advice; take the quote and the doctor's letter to a tax professional. VA HISA grants and Medicare Advantage benefits that may help are covered in our guide to paying for a parent's walk-in tub as a family.

What else should you settle before moving day?

Who will help with bathing if help is needed, and whether your parent is comfortable with that person. What time of day the bathroom is theirs. And the conversation itself: a parent who feels the bathroom was redone "for" them without being asked often resists using it. Questions families ask

How wide does a bathroom door need to be for a walker or wheelchair?

The ADA Standards require at least 32 inches of clear width, measured from the door stop to the face of the open door at 90 degrees, and 36 inches if the doorway is deeper than 24 inches. Private homes are not bound by the ADA, but 32 inches clear is the working figure. Offset hinges, an outswing door or a pocket door can add room without moving walls.

What toilet height is best for an elderly parent?

The ADA Standards specify 17 to 19 inches to the top of the seat, with 15 to 19 inches allowed in dwelling units. Higher is easier to stand from. A raised toilet seat with arms is a same-day fix; a comfort-height toilet is the permanent one. Add grab bars beside the toilet, placed where your parent's hand naturally reaches.

Should we put in a walk-in tub or a roll-in shower when my parent moves in?

In a shared home a roll-in shower usually ages best: nothing to step over, room for a chair, a walker or a helper, and the rest of the family can use it normally. Choose a walk-in tub if a warm soak is what will keep your parent bathing and they can sit while it fills and drains. Published installed prices are $13,500 to $17,000 and about $15,500 respectively.

Can I deduct bathroom modifications for a parent who lives with me?

Possibly. IRS Publication 502 allows medical expenses you paid for a dependent, and a parent may qualify if you provided over half their support. Bathroom grab bars, support bars and widened doorways made for medical reasons can usually be included in full, but only medical expenses above 7.5% of adjusted gross income count and you must itemize. Confirm with a tax professional.

How long does the installation disrupt the household?

Aging Safely Baths installs most walk-in tubs and standard 60-inch tub-to-shower conversions in one working day with a two-person crew; tile removal or moving a drain can add a second day. Plan for the family to use another bathroom that day and schedule it before your parent moves in if you can.
