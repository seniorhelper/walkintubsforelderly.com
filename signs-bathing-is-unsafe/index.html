Signs bathing is no longer safe for your parent

Older adults rarely announce that the bathtub scares them. More often they adapt quietly: shorter showers, fewer baths, a firm grip on whatever is nearby. Here is what to look for, and what each sign tells you.

Direct answer: Watch for a parent who bathes less often, grabs towel bars or the sink to steady themselves, sits on the tub edge to swing their legs over, needs their hands to stand up from a chair, or has had any fall or near-fall. Tell their doctor, ask for an occupational therapy home safety visit, and add grab bars and nonslip surfaces right away.

Published October 15, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026 · Aging Safely Baths

Why the bathroom deserves its own look

The CDC's STEADI program says more than one in four people 65 and older fall each year, and most falls happen at home. The bathroom combines nearly every risk in one small room: wet, hard surfaces, a high tub wall to step over, a low seat to get up from, and the urge to hurry to the toilet at night. The National Institute on Aging names that last one directly, noting that conditions causing a rushed trip to the bathroom can raise the chance of falling.

The good news is that the bathroom is also one of the easiest rooms to change. Spotting the problem early gives your parent more choices and gives you time to talk it over calmly.

What you might see during a visit

Signs in the bathroom itself

  • The tub looks unused. Dry soap, dust on the tub floor, or bath products moved to the sink can mean your parent has switched to sponge baths because getting in feels risky.
  • Towel bars that are loose or bent. Towel bars are not built to hold body weight. A wobbly one usually means someone has been pulling on it.
  • A bath mat that slides, or no mat at all. NIA advises nonskid mats, strips or carpet on every surface that may get wet.
  • Things stored on the tub edge or floor. Bottles on the rim become something to reach over; items on the floor become something to step around.
  • No light at night. NIA recommends leaving a bathroom light on or using a night light that turns on automatically.

Signs in how your parent moves

The CDC's Stay Independent brochure includes a 12-question fall-risk checklist developed by the Greater Los Angeles VA. Several of its questions describe exactly what you would notice at bath time:

  • Steadying themselves by holding onto furniture when walking at home
  • Needing to push with their hands to stand up from a chair, which the brochure calls a sign of weak leg muscles
  • Trouble stepping up onto a curb, the same motion as stepping over a tub wall
  • Often having to rush to the toilet
  • Saying they are worried about falling
  • Using, or having been told to use, a cane or walker

The brochure scores each answer and suggests that a total of 4 or more points means a person may be at risk of falling and should discuss it with their doctor. It is a short, plain-language form you can print and go through together, which also makes a gentle way to open the conversation.

Signs in what your parent says, or does not say

  • "I just wash up at the sink these days."
  • Bathing has moved to the days when you or a helper is around.
  • A story about "nearly slipping" that gets brushed off.
  • Changes in mood or hygiene. NIA lists whether a person is bathing regularly among the signs that an older relative may need help, and notes that depression in older people is sometimes mistaken for normal aging.

What to do this week, this month, and this year

This week

  • Move everything your parent uses in the bath to within easy reach from a seated position.
  • Put a nonslip mat inside the tub and a rubber-backed mat outside it. Remove loose rugs.
  • Add a night light between the bed and the bathroom.
  • Check the water heater. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends 120°F to lower the risk of tap water scalds.

This month

  • Book a doctor visit. The CDC's family caregiver brochure suggests bringing an up-to-date list of every medicine, including over-the-counter drugs and supplements, and asking about side effects like dizziness.
  • Ask the doctor for an occupational therapy home safety evaluation.
  • Have grab bars professionally installed: the CDC recommends them next to and inside the tub and next to the toilet.
  • Add a tub transfer bench and a handheld shower so your parent can sit to wash.

Within the year

If the signs keep adding up, the tub itself may be the problem, and no amount of equipment will lower its wall. That is when families look at a walk-in tub, a tub-to-shower conversion, or a roll-in shower. Before you shop, read our guide on how to raise it with your parent, because the best bathroom change is one your parent agreed to.

Aging Safely Baths has a good explainer on the point where bathing shifts from independence to needing help, which many families find useful to read together.

The one professional worth bringing in

An occupational therapist is trained to watch how a person moves through daily tasks like bathing and to recommend the specific equipment or changes that fit them. An OT's opinion is most useful before you choose between a walk-in tub and a shower, because the therapist can see whether your parent can manage a step-in, a seated wait, or a transfer. Some parents also accept advice from a therapist more readily than from their own children. NIA's own guide to whether an older adult needs help also suggests offering to make the doctor appointment and going along.

Questions families ask

What are the first signs that bathing is becoming unsafe for a parent?

Bathing less often, steadying themselves on towel bars, the sink or the shower door, sitting on the tub edge to swing their legs over, needing their hands to push up from a chair, and a wet or cluttered bathroom floor. Any fall or near-fall in the bathroom counts as a warning, even with no injury.

My parent says they are fine. How can I tell?

The National Institute on Aging notes that a phone call often is not enough to tell whether someone needs help. Visit, stay a few days if you can, and look at the bathroom itself: wet towels on the floor, a bath mat that slides, items stored on the tub edge, and whether the tub looks used.

Should I tell the doctor if my parent fell but was not hurt?

Yes. CDC guidance for family caregivers says to tell a healthcare provider right away if your loved one has fallen, is worried about falling, or seems unsteady. Fewer than half of older adults who fall tell their doctor.

Who can assess the bathroom professionally?

An occupational therapist. Your parent's doctor can refer them, and the visit is often covered when it is part of prescribed home health or outpatient therapy. The therapist watches how your parent actually gets in and out and recommends equipment or changes.

Does needing help with bathing mean my parent cannot live alone?

Not necessarily. Bathing is often the first daily task that gets hard, and it is one of the easiest to fix with equipment, a bathroom change or a few hours of help a week. Talk it through with your parent and their doctor before assuming a move is needed.
