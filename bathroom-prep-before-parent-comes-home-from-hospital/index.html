Bathroom changes to make before your parent comes home from the hospital

The discharge date usually arrives faster than the family expects. A hip or knee replacement, a stroke or a fall changes how your parent gets on and off the toilet and in and out of the shower, and the bathroom that worked last month may not work on Tuesday. Here is what you can realistically change in two days, and what takes two weeks.

Direct answer: In 48 hours you can add a shower chair or tub transfer bench, a handheld shower, a raised toilet seat or 3-in-1 commode, a non-slip mat and night lights, and remove rugs. Grab bars screwed into studs take a few days to arrange. A roll-in shower ($13,500 to $17,000 installed) or walk-in tub (about $15,500 installed after the current promotion) is usually a one-day install once scheduled.

Published November 8, 2026 · Updated November 8, 2026 · By the Aging Safely Baths family guide team

Who tells you what the bathroom needs?

Start with the discharge planner, and use Medicare's own "Your Discharge Planning Checklist" as your script. It tells patients and caregivers to ask where care will happen after discharge, whether medical equipment such as a walker is needed, whether Medicare covers it and who will arrange delivery, and to circle the daily activities the patient still needs help with, including bathing and using the bathroom.

Ask specifically for an occupational therapist's recommendations for the bathroom. If your parent qualifies for Medicare home health after discharge, you pay nothing for covered home health services, which can include occupational and physical therapy; a home health aide is covered only alongside skilled nursing or therapy, and Medicare does not cover personal care such as bathing when that is the only care needed. Our bathroom safety assessment guide explains what a therapist checks and the other ways to get one into the house.

What changes depending on why your parent was in the hospital?

  • Hip replacement. Kaiser Permanente Washington's occupational therapy checklist says toilet height matters, the higher the easier, and that posterior-approach hip precautions require a 3-in-1 commode or raised toilet seat. It also asks whether the bathroom door is wide enough for the walker and whether the bathroom is on the same level as the bedroom; if not, it suggests staying on the bathroom's level for the first few days or using a bedside commode.
  • Knee replacement. The same checklist applies: seated showering, a transfer bench if the tub wall must be crossed, a handheld shower and a long-handled sponge so your parent does not bend to reach their feet.
  • Stroke. One side may be weaker, so where the grab bars go and which way your parent transfers matters. Ask the therapist to mark the wall before anyone drills.
  • A fall. The CDC notes that falling once doubles the chance of falling again, and fear of falling is itself one of the 12 items on the CDC's Stay Independent screen. Run our fall risk check with your parent so the family and the doctor are working from the same score.

What can you do in 48 hours, and what takes two weeks?

Hospital for Special Surgery's home-preparation guide covers the quick items: a night light in the bathroom, nonskid tape or mats at the sink, rugs anchored or removed, and someone in the house for the first 24 hours. What if there is not enough time before discharge?

Then plan for the first two weeks, not the next ten years. Set your parent up on the same floor as a usable bathroom, or use a bedside commode (Kaiser Permanente's advice for the first few days). Sponge baths and a seated wash at the sink are fine until the equipment and the therapist's visit are in place; our guide to helping a parent bathe with dignity covers the setup. Book the permanent change after the first follow-up visit, when you know how your parent is actually moving.

Who pays for the bathroom changes?

Original Medicare generally does not pay for walk-in tubs or showers, because they are home modifications rather than durable medical equipment. Some Medicare Advantage plans, state Medicaid waivers and the VA's HISA grant (up to $6,800 for a service-connected need, $2,000 otherwise) may help, and medical home modifications may count toward the IRS medical deduction. Treat each as "may qualify" until checked. Our guide to paying for a parent's walk-in tub as a family covers splitting the cost, the tax rules and the paperwork.

Can the work happen while your parent is still in rehab?

Often, yes, and a rehab stay is the easiest time for a one-day install because nobody needs the bathroom that day. Someone must be home to let the crew in. Questions families ask

What bathroom equipment does a parent need after a hip replacement?

Therapists most often recommend a raised toilet seat with arms or a 3-in-1 commode, a shower chair or tub transfer bench, a handheld shower head, a non-slip mat, grab bars, and a long-handled sponge. Kaiser Permanente Washington's checklist lists a raised seat or commode as required after a posterior-approach hip replacement. Confirm the list with your parent's own therapist.

Will Medicare pay for a shower chair or grab bars?

Ask the discharge planner; Medicare's own checklist tells you to ask whether equipment is covered and who arranges delivery. Original Medicare generally does not pay for bathroom modifications such as grab bars, walk-in tubs or showers. Some Medicare Advantage plans and state Medicaid waivers may help, so check before you buy.

How fast can a walk-in tub or roll-in shower be installed?

The installation itself is usually one working day. Aging Safely Baths says a standard 60-inch tub-to-shower conversion takes one day with a two-person crew, with tile removal or moving the drain adding a second day. The limiting factor is delivery of the unit, so call as soon as you have a discharge date.

Should we buy the big change before we know how my parent recovers?

Not always. If recovery is expected to be full, a transfer bench and grab bars may be all you need, and you can decide on a permanent change after the first follow-up visit. If the fall or stroke revealed a longer-term decline, scheduling the permanent change during the rehab stay avoids a second disruption.

Does Medicare cover a home health aide to help with bathing?

Only alongside skilled care. Medicare.gov says a home health aide is covered when your parent is also getting skilled nursing, physical therapy, speech-language pathology or occupational therapy, and that personal care such as bathing is not covered when it is the only care needed. You pay nothing for covered home health services.
